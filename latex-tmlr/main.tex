\begin{abstract}
Quantizing a proposer's weights can leave every metric a discovery loop watches unchanged while collapsing the variation the loop depends on. On a constructive-geometry task at 14B, degrading served quantization leaves viability and validity flat, while at the 2-bit rung the proposer largely stops departing from its parent. An echo bound released before the run that tested it held on five never-sampled seeds --- \textbf{79\% (19/24)} coordinate-verified parent-echo against \textbf{6\% (1/17)}; 92\% against 33\% once parent quality is held fixed --- and a probe explicitly instructing the model not to copy returned coordinate-identical outputs in \textbf{5 of 5} valid rows. The failed proposals are coherent and well-formed, which is why pass/fail instruments report health. The cost is visible only at the loop level: the 2-bit rung takes 1 accepted improvement in 50 calls against 14--16 at the upper rungs (a post-hoc statistic), while final best score does not separate the rungs at the power available. A sixth model family, screen-selected at 31B after five others fell below registered competence floors, reproduces the search collapse under a registered floor-gated test --- 0.0 accepted steps against 4.8, with viability intact at both rungs. We then argue that served quantization is one of several serving-path variables an alias-addressed managed runtime leaves unattested, and document a forensic case where an alias drifted within six days --- byte-identical prompts returning 30/30 valid against the original 4/30 --- and took the question with it. We close with a cheap repair protocol: prompt hashing before sampling, verbatim raw storage, dated alias maps, hash-locked preregistration.
\end{abstract}

\subsubsection*{Broader Impact Statement}

This paper reports a way for a quantized proposer to keep every health metric a
discovery loop watches while the search underneath it collapses. Two readings
would be misuses of that result. It is not an argument against quantization:
quantized serving is what puts capable models within reach of researchers
without datacentre budgets, and nothing here shows harm at the rungs most
practitioners use --- the collapse we measure is at the 2-bit endpoint, on one
task family, at one scale. Nor is it an argument that published loop results are
wrong; they are unrepeatable in a specific and now-demonstrable way, which
calls for disclosure rather than retraction. The costs of the repair protocol in
\S6 fall hardest on exactly the researchers least able to absorb them, and we
have tried to keep every item in it cheap and local.

Two further repercussions are worth naming. The forensic method of \S4 --- reading
a serving path from latency and behaviour --- is a capability as well as a
diagnostic, and the same reasoning that lets a researcher ask what served their
calls could let a third party probe an endpoint its operator would rather not
have characterized; we think the transparency gain dominates here, because the
alternative is a research literature that cannot audit its own substrate, but
the direction is not costless. Finally, this work addresses a commercial alias
whose behaviour changed mid-study. We report that as an observability failure of
the addressing mode, common to managed runtimes generally, and not as a claim
about any vendor's conduct; we have no evidence of intent and make no
allegation of any.
